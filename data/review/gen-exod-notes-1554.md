# Genesis and Exodus: footnotes from Castellio's 1554 annotations (10)

Proposed 2026-11-16, by the method used for Leviticus (`data/review/lev-notes-1554.md`, `lev23-24-note.md`): every
annotation on Genesis and Exodus in the 1554 Basel edition was read on the page and sorted into (a) deliberate
departures from the Hebrew as read today, (b) words no dictionary has that Castellio explains himself, and (c)
commentary or defences of Latin a reader can follow (no note). Decisions 53 and 56; brief.md, "brief Latin
footnotes". Each note sits right after its word; the Hebrew is the WLC's (`sources/parallel/wlc`) without the
cantillation marks.

**Where the annotations are** (MDZ bsb10196330; images `https://api.digitale-sammlungen.de/iiif/image/v2/bsb10196330_00NNN/…`,
read 2026-11-16): *Annotationes*, preface and *In Genesim* from canvas 805 (columns 305–306) to canvas 811 (column
317); *In Exodum* from canvas 811 (column 317) to canvas 820 (column 335), where *In Leviticum* begins. Canvases
813–819 are mostly the tabernacle woodcuts (ark, table, lampstand, ground plan, frame, curtains, altar grate, altar,
high priest) with Castellio's explanations of them. In all, after a preface on method, 69 annotations on Genesis
and 29 on Exodus (counting the tabernacle explanations as one); the full list, in the page's order, is at the end.

**Quotations** follow the page: u/v and i/j as printed, the tilde for a nasal (*mendũ*), *s* for ſ (*ß* written
*ss*). **Modern renderings** were surveyed on Bible Gateway's all-translations page for each verse and in the JPS
translations on Sefaria (the gender-sensitive revised JPS of 2023, the NJPS of 1985, the JPS of 1917), 2026-11-16.

The settled text (1738) still has Castellio's reading at all ten places. Exod 17:16, in the first draft, is dropped: his
reading matches a modern one (see the end).

## 1. Gen 4:23 *sī quem mortālem vulneribus interficerem*

The Hebrew כִּי אִישׁ הָרַגְתִּי is a perfect, "for I have slain a man" (Vulgate *quoniam occidi virum*). Castellio
(canvas 807, columns 309–310, *EX CAP. IIII.*): *Si quem ego hominem plagis.) … Homicidium ita nefariũ habetur Deo, ut
statuerit, si quis etiam Cainum, hominem fratricidam, occiderit, eum tãto esse supplicio dignum, ut si septies mori
poßit, septies sit interficiendus. Quid ergo æquum est fieri in cæde hominis non homicidæ? … Quod autem uerto, Si
interficerẽ, cum sit in Hebræo præteritum: sciendum est, Hebræos præterito uti pro coniunctiuo, ut & alibi fit, & Iob.3.
& Psal.44. & Iud.8. & Ierem.37.* So Lamech does not boast of a killing; he states what a murder of an innocent
man deserves.

Modern renderings: every modern English version on Bible Gateway has a past deed ("I have killed a man for wounding
me", NRSVue, NIV, ESV, NET, NABRE, NASB, CSB…), as do the JPS translations ("I have slain a man for wounding me");
only versions in the Geneva line have a conditional (Geneva 1599 "I would slay a man", RGT "I would kill", JUB "I
shall slay").

> *Hebraicē כִּי אִישׁ הָרַגְתִּי, ‘nam virum occīdī’; Castelliō, præteritum prō conjūnctīvō positum ratus, Lamēchum nōn suum facinus nārrāre, sed in ūniversum loquī intellegit.*

("Hebrew 'for I have killed a man'; Castellio, taking the perfect to stand for a subjunctive, understands that Lamech
is not telling of his own deed but speaking in general.")

Revised 2026-11-16 (Nathan: *sententiam generālem* "reads as an English calque"). *In ūniversum*, "in general", is
Latin of Seneca, Pliny the Younger, Quintilian and Tacitus (`latin attest "in universum"`: 96 hits; e.g. Seneca,
*Epistulae morales* (Latin Library, books 14–15), *nec in universum componit hominem*; Pliny, *Ep.* 10.113, *in universum a me non potest statui*).

**Decision:** accept (Nathan, 2026-11-16: "Accept all except 4")
**Apply:** `Gen.4.23` la: `sī quem mortālem vulneribus interficerem,` → `sī quem mortālem vulneribus interficerem\f + \fr 4:23 \ft Hebraicē כִּי אִישׁ הָרַגְתִּי, ‘nam virum occīdī’; Castelliō, præteritum prō conjūnctīvō positum ratus, Lamēchum nōn suum facinus nārrāre, sed in ūniversum loquī intellegit.\f*,`

## 2. Gen 6:2 *hominum potentissimī*

The Hebrew בְנֵי־הָאֱלֹהִים is "the sons of God" (Vulgate *filii Dei*). Castellio (canvas 807, column 310, *EX CAP.
VI.*): *Hominum potentissimi.) In Hebræo est, Deorũ filij. Sed utuntur substantiuis sæpe pro adiectiuis, quæ rariora
habent: ut sint Deorum filij, pro diuinis: id est, præstantibus. ut etiã in Psal.82. dij siue diui uocantur iudices,
& aliorum gubernatores.* He goes on to *Filij* as "men" (*filij Israel, id est homines*). The text follows him in 6:4
(*ex illīs potentum cōnūbiīs*).

Modern renderings: most keep "the sons of God" (KJV, NRSVue, ESV, NIV, NASB, NET, NABRE…); some say "divine beings" or
"heavenly beings" (CEB, ISV, GNT; the NJPS and revised JPS "the divine beings"). The JPS of 1917 has "the sons of
nobles", close to Castellio.

**Modern scholarship, looked at more closely (2026-11-16):**
- The NET's note gives three readings: (1) angelic beings, as the phrase plainly means in Job 1:6, 2:1, 38:7, and
  because the text sets the "sons of God" apart from humankind; (2) Seth's line; (3) "powerful tyrants … who viewed
  themselves as divine". It argues against (2) and (3) ("usage of the phrase … in Job militates against this view"),
  citing Wenham, *Genesis* (WBC) 1:135.
- Wikipedia's article "Sons of God" (read 2026-11-16): "All of the earliest sources interpret the Genesis 6:2 'sons of
  God' as supernatural beings and angels" (1 Enoch, Jubilees, the Dead Sea Scrolls, Josephus); the "merely human"
  reading is that of "Rabbinic sources, most notably the Targum" (citing Payne: Targum Onkelos and Jonathan, and
  Symmachus, "sons of aristocratic families"), and of Saadia, Rashi and Ibn Ezra. Its critical scholars (Westermann,
  Hendel, Parker) all treat the passage as myth about divine beings.
- A 2025 article, "The Problems of Sons of Gods, Daughters of Humans, and the Nephilim in Genesis 6:1–4: A
  Reassessment" (*Religions* 16, 972), takes "the union between divine beings and human women" as given, without
  arguing it.

So Castellio's reading is the rabbinic one (Targum, Symmachus); modern critical scholarship, and the JPS
translations, read divine beings. I have not seen a count that would justify *plērīque*, so the note says *multī*.

> *Hebraicē בְנֵי־הָאֱלֹהִים, ‘fīliī Deī’, quōs hodiē multī caelitēs intellegunt; Castelliō, ‘fīliōs’ prō adjectīvō positōs ratus, hominēs præstantēs.*

("Hebrew 'the sons of God', whom many now take to be heavenly beings; Castellio, taking 'sons' to stand for an
adjective, understands outstanding men.")

**Decision:** accept (Nathan, 2026-11-16: "Accept all except 4")
**Apply:** `Gen.6.2` la: `hominum potentissimī, ēligēbant` → `hominum potentissimī\f + \fr 6:2 \ft Hebraicē בְנֵי־הָאֱלֹהִים, ‘fīliī Deī’, quōs hodiē multī caelitēs intellegunt; Castelliō, ‘fīliōs’ prō adjectīvō positōs ratus, hominēs præstantēs.\f*, ēligēbant`

## 3. Gen 33:19 *centum quessitis* (kind b)

The Hebrew קְשִׂיטָה is a unit of money of unknown value (NJPS and revised JPS "a hundred kesitahs", with the note "a
unit of unknown value"; KJV "pieces of money"; Vulgate *centum agnis*). Castellio keeps the Hebrew word as Latin and
explains it (canvas 809, column 314, *EX CAP. XXXIII.*, margin *Quessita.*): *Centum quessitis emit.) Quessitam fuisse
nummum puto non uilem, id quod tum ex hoc loco conijcio, tum ex Iobi capite ultimo, ubi scriptum est, Iobo amicos
singulas dedisse quessitas.* Lewis & Short and the Neo-Latin Lexicon have no *quessita*: a reader cannot look it up.
Like *monētum*, a word no dictionary has that Castellio explains himself; here a Hebrew word kept, not a coinage.

> *Hebraicē קְשִׂיטָה, nummus cujus pretium hodiē ignōrātur; Castelliō vōcem retinet.*

("Hebrew *qesitah*, a coin whose value is unknown today; Castellio keeps the word.")

Revised 2026-11-16 (Nathan): his guess that the coin was not a cheap one corrects nothing in the translation, so it
goes. *Vōx* for "word" is classical: Lewis & Short, *vox* II.A, "that which is uttered by the voice, i.e. a word,
saying" (32 quotations, Cicero among them).

**Decision:** accept (Nathan, 2026-11-16: "Accept all except 4")
**Apply:** `Gen.33.19` la: `centum quessitis ēmit:` → `centum quessitis\f + \fr 33:19 \ft Hebraicē קְשִׂיטָה, nummus cujus pretium hodiē ignōrātur; Castelliō vōcem retinet.\f* ēmit:`

## 4. Gen 48:20 *Per vōs*

The Hebrew בְּךָ יְבָרֵךְ יִשְׂרָאֵל is singular, "by thee shall Israel bless" (Vulgate *In te benedicetur Israel*).
Castellio (canvas 810, column 315, *EX CAP. XLVIII.*): *Per uos bene precentur Israelitæ.) In Hebræo est, Per te. Sed
cum utrique dicatur bene ominari, & Græcè legatur ἐν ὑμῖν, malui per pluralem uertere.* (Brenton's Septuagint: ἐν
ὑμῖν εὐλογηθήσεται Ἰσραήλ.) A deliberate departure, with the Greek against the Hebrew.

**Modern scholarship:** the NET keeps the singular ("By you will Israel bless") and notes: "The pronoun is singular in
the Hebrew text, apparently elevating Ephraim as the more prominent of the two. Note, however, that both are named
in the blessing formula that follows." So the number is read today as meaningful, which Castellio's plural removes.
English "you" hides the number in most versions; Latin cannot.

> *Hebraicē בְּךָ, ‘per tē’, singulāriter; Castelliō, quod utrīque bene precātur, Græcōs sequitur, quī ἐν ὑμῖν habent.*

("Hebrew 'by thee', in the singular; Castellio, because both are blessed, follows the Greek, which has 'by you'
(plural).")

**Decision:** reject (Nathan, 2026-11-16: "We can probably drop this")
**Apply:** `Gen.48.20` la: `“Per vōs,” inquit,` → `“Per vōs\f + \fr 48:20 \ft Hebraicē בְּךָ, ‘per tē’, singulāriter; Castelliō, quod utrīque bene precātur, Græcōs sequitur, quī ἐν ὑμῖν habent.\f*,” inquit,`

## 5. Gen 49:6 *mūrum subruerint*

The Hebrew וּבִרְצֹנָם עִקְּרוּ־שׁוֹר is pointed *shor*, "ox": "in their self-will they hamstrung an ox". Read *shur*,
the same letters mean "wall", as the Vulgate (*suffoderunt murum*) and the KJV ("digged down a wall") have it; the
Septuagint has ἐνευροκόπησαν ταῦρον, "they hamstrung a bull". Castellio has *mūrum subruerint* and explains it without
arguing the reading (canvas 810, column 316): *Quod autẽ dicit, Murũ subruerint: significat eorũ nefariã in Sichemã
irruptionem.*

**Modern renderings:** every modern version on Bible Gateway has the animal: "oxen" (NRSVue, NIV, ESV, NASB, NET, NABRE,
CSB, NLT, NKJV "an ox"…), "cattle" (CJB, GW, ISV, LEB, WEB), "bulls" (GNT); "wall" only in the KJV and its revisions,
the Douay-Rheims, the Geneva Bible, Wycliffe, JUB and RGT. The JPS: NJPS "maim oxen", the revised JPS "maim an ox".
Not argued by Castellio, but different from the Hebrew as read today, which Nathan has decided is enough here
(2026-11-16: "Gen 49:6 does need a note since it differs from modern scholarship, even if he didn't argue for it").
*Subnervāre* is the Latin for "to hamstring" (Lewis & Short, with *taurum* among its examples).

> *Hebraicē עִקְּרוּ־שׁוֹר, ‘taurum subnervārunt’; Castelliō, ut Hierōnymus, שׁוּר, ‘mūrum’, legit.*

("Hebrew 'they hamstrung an ox'; Castellio, like Jerome, reads *shur*, 'wall'.")

**Decision:** accept (Nathan, 2026-11-16: "Accept all except 4")
**Apply:** `Gen.49.6` la: `et libīdine mūrum subruerint.` → `et libīdine mūrum\f + \fr 49:6 \ft Hebraicē עִקְּרוּ־שׁוֹר, ‘taurum subnervārunt’; Castelliō, ut Hierōnymus, שׁוּר, ‘mūrum’, legit.\f* subruerint.`

## 6. Gen 49:10 *dōnec veniat Sospitātor*

The Hebrew עַד כִּי־יָבֹא שילה (written שילה, read שִׁילֹה) is one of the hardest places in Genesis (Vulgate *donec
veniat qui mittendus est*). Castellio (canvas 810, column 316, *EX CAP. XLIX.*): *Quod deinde sequitur, Donec ueniat
sospitator: in Hebræo est שילה, quod à שלה deductũ, significat sospitalẽ, & felicitatis autorẽ: id est, CHRISTVM IESVM
Dominũ nostrũ, de Iudæ semine creatũ: cuius in aduentu regnũ ad Herodẽ, hominẽ alienigenam, id est, Idumæum,
transierat …* (margin *Sospitator*). *Sospitātor* is a Latin word (Lewis & Short: "a saviour", post-classical, of
Christ).

Modern renderings, three ways: "until Shiloh comes" (KJV, NASB, NKJV, LSB, LEB); "until he comes to whom it belongs"
(RSV, NIV, NET, CSB, NLT); "until tribute comes to him" (ESV, NRSVue, NABRE; NJPS and revised JPS "so that tribute
shall come to him", noting "meaning of Heb. uncertain"). The Amplified Bible glosses Shiloh "[the Messiah, the
Peaceful One]", nearest Castellio's derivation. No version surveyed has "Saviour".

> *Hebraicē שילה, vōx obscūra, quam hodiē aliī ‘cujus est’, aliī ‘tribūtum eī’ legunt; Castelliō ā שלה ductam sospitālem, id est Chrīstum, intellegit.*

("Hebrew *shiloh*, an obscure word, which some now read 'whose it is', others 'tribute to him'; Castellio takes it
from *shalah* to mean a saviour, that is Christ.")

**Decision:** accept (Nathan, 2026-11-16: "Accept all except 4")
**Apply:** `Gen.49.10` la: `dōnec veniat Sospitātor,` → `dōnec veniat Sospitātor\f + \fr 49:10 \ft Hebraicē שילה, vōx obscūra, quam hodiē aliī ‘cujus est’, aliī ‘tribūtum eī’ legunt; Castelliō ā שלה ductam sospitālem, id est Chrīstum, intellegit.\f*,`

## 7. Exod 19:23 *certōs eī terminōs statuī*

The Hebrew הַגְבֵּל אֶת־הָהָר וְקִדַּשְׁתּוֹ is "set bounds about the mountain and sanctify it" (Vulgate *Pone terminos
circa montem*). Castellio (canvas 812, column 319, *EX CAP. XIX.*): *Certos ei terminos statuimus.) Ad uerbum sic
legitur, Tu nos admonuisti his uerbis: Defini montem, eumq; lustra. Atqui non montem Deus definiri & lustrari iusserat
paulo ante, sed populum: itaque non ההר, sed העם legendum uidetur.* In 19:12 the Hebrew has וְהִגְבַּלְתָּ אֶת־הָעָם
(Castellio *Tū iīs terminum circumscrībitō*). So the *eī* and *eum* of his 19:23 are the people, not the mountain;
the reader, seeing *montem Sīnam* just before, will take them of the mountain. (1554 *statuimus*, 1738 *statuī*.)

Modern renderings: every version surveyed, and the JPS, has the mountain ("Set limits around the mountain and keep it
holy", NRSVue).

**Modern scholarship, looked at more closely (2026-11-16):** the tension Castellio saw is known, but it is resolved
the other way, at 19:12, not 19:23:
- Utley's *Free Bible Commentary* on 19:12: "The Samaritan Pentateuch has 'mountain.' Both could fit the context."
  (I have not seen the Samaritan text itself.)
- The translators' notes at tips.translation.bible on 19:12: the Hebrew is literally "you will delimit the people";
  "mountain" is not in the text, but the context supplies it, and NABRE, NIV and GNT add it ("Set limits for the
  people all around the mountain", NABRE). The same page says the Septuagint reads "the mountain" there; Brenton's
  Septuagint has τὸν λαόν at 19:12, so that depends on the edition.
- At 19:23 the Septuagint (Brenton) agrees with the Hebrew: ἀφόρισαι τὸ ὄρος. The NET's notes on 19:23 raise no
  textual question.
No source seen reads "people" at 19:23. Castellio's emendation goes against all the witnesses and modern readings.

> *Hebraicē הַגְבֵּל אֶת־הָהָר, ‘terminā montem’; Castelliō, quia 19:12 populus terminārī jubētur, הָעָם, ‘populum’, legit.*

("Hebrew 'set bounds about the mountain'; Castellio, because in 19:12 the people are to be bounded, reads 'the
people'.")

**Decision:** accept (Nathan, 2026-11-16: "Accept all except 4")
**Apply:** `Exod.19.23` la: `certōs eī terminōs statuī, eumque` → `certōs eī terminōs statuī\f + \fr 19:23 \ft Hebraicē הַגְבֵּל אֶת־הָהָר, ‘terminā montem’; Castelliō, quia 19:12 populus terminārī jubētur, הָעָם, ‘populum’, legit.\f*, eumque`

## 8. Exod 23:15 *Azȳmālia* (kind b)

Castellio names the three feasts with words of his own (canvas 812, column 320, *EX CAP. XXIII.*): *Azymalia
seruato.) Sicut Latini ueteres agonalia, floralia, liberalia, & cætera dixerũt, ego mihi fas putaui, azymorum festum
azymalia dicere, messis messalia, conditionis conditalia.* None is in Lewis & Short or the Neo-Latin Lexicon. This is
the first place in reading order; *azȳmālia* recurs in Exod 34:18, Lev 23 and the Gospels (Matt 26:17, Mark 14:1, 12,
which have no note), *conditālia* in Exod 34:22. The note goes here only, like *monētum*'s.

> *‘Azȳmālia’, ‘messālia’ et ‘conditālia’ (23:16) Castelliō fīnxit, ut Agōnālia, Flōrālia, Līberālia.*

("Castellio coined *azymalia*, *messalia* and *conditalia*, like *Agonalia*, *Floralia*, *Liberalia*.")

Revised 2026-11-16 (Nathan asked whether the capitals are regular and whether *Latīnī veterēs* is natural). *Latīnī
veterēs* is Castellio's own phrase (*Sicut Latini ueteres agonalia, floralia …*), but the note needs no subject at
all; the Lev 23:24 note has the same shape, *ut Jūnō Monēta*. The Roman festivals are names and take capitals, as in
Lewis & Short and Gaffiot (*Agōnālia*, *Līberālia*); like *Monēta* they are named, not quoted, so they lose the quotation
marks. Castellio's three words keep the edition's lower case (*messālia*, *conditālia* in 23:16 and 34:22; *Azȳmālia*
has a capital in 23:15 only because it begins the verse), and they are quoted as words.

**Decision:** accept (Nathan, 2026-11-16: "Accept all except 4")
**Apply:** `Exod.23.15` la: `Azȳmālia servātō,` → `Azȳmālia\f + \fr 23:15 \ft ‘Azȳmālia’, ‘messālia’ et ‘conditālia’ (23:16) Castelliō fīnxit, ut Agōnālia, Flōrālia, Līberālia.\f* servātō,`

## 9. Exod 25:5 *ligna cedrina*

The Hebrew עֲצֵי שִׁטִּים is "acacia wood" (Vulgate *ligna setim*). Castellio (canvas 812, column 320, *EX CAP. XXV.*,
margin *Cedrus.*): *Ligna cedrina.) עצים שטים tradũt nonnulli ligna esse cedrina: & rectè, sicut ego iudico. Cum sit
enim cedrus & odore suauis, & tempore æterna, & cariem nunquam sentiat, nõ uideo quo ligno melius hæc fieri potuerint.
Græci quoque uerterũt ξύλα ἄσηπτα, ligna imputrefacta: quod idem sequutus est Iosephus, quæ natura nulli magis
cõuenit quàm cedro.* He renders שִׁטִּים *cedrina* throughout the tabernacle chapters; the note goes on the first.

Modern renderings: "acacia" in every modern version surveyed and the JPS; "shittim" in KJV and its revisions; "cedar"
only in the Jubilee Bible 2000.

> *Hebraicē עֲצֵי שִׁטִּים, ‘ligna acaciæ’; Castelliō cedrum intellegit, quæ cariem numquam sentit (Græcī ξύλα ἄσηπτα).*

("Hebrew 'acacia wood'; Castellio understands cedar, which never rots (the Greek: 'incorruptible wood').")

**How sure the evidence is** (Nathan asked, 2026-11-16):
- Brown-Driver-Briggs, שִׁטָּה (biblehub.com, Strong's 7848): "acacia (mimosa) Nilotica", also *a. seyyâl*, "growing in
  dry places", probably an Egyptian loanword.
- Isa 41:19 names the tree among those God will plant "in the wilderness" (בַּמִּדְבָּר): אֶרֶז שִׁטָּה וַהֲדַס, "the
  cedar, the shittah tree, and the myrtle". So שִׁטָּה is a desert tree and is not אֶרֶז, the cedar. (Castellio agrees
  it is not אֶרֶז: he takes it for a second kind of cedar, *cedrelate*, *Nam altera ארז uocatur*.)
- The tabernacle was built in the Sinai wilderness. Bible-reference sites and translators' notes (secondary, via a
  search summary) name *Acacia tortilis*, *A. raddiana* and *A. seyal* as native to the wadis of Sinai, the Negev and
  the Arabah; I have not seen a botanical source.
- Every modern version surveyed and the JPS have "acacia".
- Castellio's grounds are qualities (fragrance, lasting, never rotting), the Greek ξύλα ἄσηπτα, "incorruptible wood",
  which names no tree, and Josephus following the Greek.
So the identification is sound: a dictionary and Isaiah's own wording, against Castellio's reasoning from qualities.

**Decision:** accept (Nathan, 2026-11-16: "Accept all except 4")
**Apply:** `Exod.25.5` la: `pellēs mēlium, ligna cedrina,` → `pellēs mēlium, ligna cedrina\f + \fr 25:5 \ft Hebraicē עֲצֵי שִׁטִּים, ‘ligna acaciæ’; Castelliō cedrum intellegit, quæ cariem numquam sentit (Græcī ξύλα ἄσηπτα).\f*,`

## 10. Exod 30:24 *Īris quīngentōs*

The Hebrew קִדָּה is "cassia" (Vulgate *casiæ*). Castellio (canvas 820, column 335, *EX CAP. XXX.*, margin *Casia.*):
*Iris quingentos.) קדה casiam interpretantur. Sed casiam Hebræi eodem nomine, quo Latini appellant, ut est in
Psal.45. Itaq; malui hic Iosephum imitari.* (Ps 45:9 has קְצִיעוֹת.) As with *perseæ* at Lev 23:40, he follows Josephus
against the Hebrew. The annotation does not quote Josephus, so the note does not say what Josephus wrote.

Modern renderings: "cassia" in nearly every version surveyed and the JPS ("cinnamon" in AMP, CEV, MSG).

> *Hebraicē קִדָּה, ‘casia’; Castelliō, quod casiam Hebraicē קְצִיעוֹת vocārī putat (Psalmō 45), Jōsēphum sequitur, quī īrim nōminat.*

("Hebrew 'cassia'; Castellio, because he thinks the Hebrew for cassia is *qetsiʿot* (Psalm 45), follows Josephus, who
names iris.")

Revised 2026-11-16 (Nathan: "I don't really understand this note"). Castellio's argument, spelled out: translators
render קִדָּה "cassia"; but Hebrew already has a word for cassia that sounds like the Latin one, קְצִיעוֹת in Ps 45:9
(*casiam Hebræi eodem nomine, quo Latini appellant*); so קִדָּה must be something else, and he takes Josephus's
word. Josephus, *Ant.* 3.197 (Perseus, read 2026-11-16): σμύρνης ἐπιλέκτου σίκλους πεντακοσίους καὶ ἴρεως ἴσους,
"five hundred shekels of choice myrrh and as many of iris": iris where Exodus has קִדָּה, with the same weight. Modern
readers take both words as cassia-type spices (Exod 30:24 and Ezek 27:19 קִדָּה, "cassia" in the KJV; Ps 45:9
קְצִיעוֹת, "cassia" in modern versions), so having a second word does not make קִדָּה iris. The note now names the
Hebrew word and Josephus's word, so the reader can follow it.

**Two words for one spice?** (Nathan asked, 2026-11-16; Sefaria and biblehub read the same day.)
- Jewish tradition takes them as the same spice. Targum Onkelos renders Exod 30:24's קִדָּה by וּקְצִיעֲתָא, and
  Pseudo-Jonathan by וּקְצִיעָתָא. Rashi: וקדה. שם שורש עשב, ובלשון חכמים קציעה, "the name of the root of a plant;
  in the language of the Sages, *qetsiʿah*". Ibn Ezra: מהקבלה ידענו שהיא קציעה, "we know from tradition that it is
  *qetsiʿah*" (his translators cite Keritot 6a).
- BDB, קִדָּה: "a spice, usually cassia"; root "dubious; perhaps foreign word"; it compares Dioscorides 1.12 (κασσία =
  ἡ κιττώ). קְצִיעָה: "cassia, a powdered bark, like cinnamon", "(hence Greek κασία, Latin casia …)". So the modern
  dictionary agrees with Castellio that קְצִיעָה is the word behind Latin *casia*, and leaves קִדָּה less certain
  ("usually cassia").
- No source seen draws a distinction between them. (The topical lexicon on biblehub says "the same spice in a different
  form", but gives no evidence, and it is not a scholarly source.)
So the evidence is for two words for one spice, an older word and the one later Hebrew used (Rashi's "language of
the Sages"). The uncertainty is in קִדָּה, but it points to cassia, not iris. The note stands.

**Decision:** accept (Nathan, 2026-11-16: "Accept all except 4")

## Not proposed

**Exod 17:16** *Quia Jōvæ manus signō admōta est* (in the first draft as item 5): Castellio emends כֵּס, "throne", to
נס, "banner" (*Nam quod in Hebræo legitur כס mendũ est …*). His reading matches modern ones: RSV, NRSV(ue) ("A hand
upon the banner of the LORD!"), NABRE ("Take up the banner of the LORD!"), CEB and GNT. Others have "throne" (ESV,
NIV, NET, JPS, with "meaning of Heb. *kes* uncertain") or "sworn" (KJV, NASB). Dropped (Nathan, 2026-11-16: "Exod
17:16 doesn't need a note if his translation already matches modern scholarship").

**Gen 6:14** *ex terebinthinā māteriā*, looked at more closely 2026-11-16. גֹּפֶר occurs only here, and its meaning is
unknown:
- NET note: "While the exact nature of the wood involved is uncertain (cf. NLT 'resinous wood'), many modern
  translations render the Hebrew term as 'cypress' (so NEB, NIV, NRSV)"; KJV, NAB, NASB and the JPS transliterate
  "gopher wood".
- The *Encyclopedia of the Bible* (Bible Gateway, "Gopher Wood", via a search summary) mentions an Akkadian cognate
  *kupru* "pitch" and a proposal to read *kopher* "pitch", but says the meaning "still remains dubious".
- Ancient versions: Septuagint ξύλων τετραγώνων "squared timbers", Vulgate *lignis lævigatis* "planed wood". The
  Targums' "cedar" is reported in older commentaries (Gill), which I have not checked.
Castellio himself says *neque tamen de terebintho prorsus affirmo, sed certius nihil habeo*. The terebinth is a
resinous tree, which fits the NLT's "resinous wood"; "cypress" is a guess too. There is no settled modern reading to
depart from. No note.

**Gen 37:36** *Pharaōnis eunūchō, magistrō equitum*, looked at more closely 2026-11-16. שַׂר הַטַּבָּחִים is literally
"chief of the slaughterers"; the Septuagint has ἀρχιμάγειρος, "chief cook", as Josephus does. Modern versions:
"captain of the guard" (KJV, WEB, BSB, NET, JPS 1917); NJPS "chief steward"; the revised JPS "his prefect", noting
"Precise force of Heb. *sar haṭṭabaḥim* uncertain" (the Contemporary Torah adds "Apparently the office was obscure
even to the text's …", the rest of its note not read); the NET adds that "the expression … might indicate that Potiphar was the chief
executioner". Search summaries report Onkelos, Saadia and Ibn Ezra reading
"chief executioner" (not checked). Castellio rejects the cooks for the same reason modern readers do: in 2 Kgs 25 the
same title belongs to Nebuzaradan, a general who burns Jerusalem (*quod munus profectò nõ est magistri laniorum, aut
coquorum*). His *magister equitum* gives that sense, a high military officer, in a Roman title; the "horse" is his
Latin, not a reading. No note, unless Nathan wants one on the Roman title: draft *Hebraicē שַׂר הַטַּבָּחִים, ‘prīnceps
satellitum’; Castelliō Rōmānō nōmine ‘magistrum equitum’ vertit.*

**Exod 20:4** *Simulācrum ūllīus reī quæ exstet*: he reads Deut 5:8's text, without the *and* of Exodus (*emendatius
legitur Deuteron.5 … sine coniunctione*); modern versions give the same sense ("an idol, whether in the form of
anything", NRSVue; "an image in the form of anything", NIV). No note.
