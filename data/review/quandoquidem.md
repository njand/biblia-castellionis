# *quandōquidem* or *quandoquidem*? What decides the o

Researched 2026-11-10 at Nathan's request (his print Gaffiot gives both quantities; the Gaffiot 2016 database used by
the pipeline gives only *quandŏquĭdem*). Nothing is applied: the settled text writes *quandōquidem* 21 times (Matt 3,
Mark 1, Luke 5, John 4, Acts 1, Rom 1, 1 Cor 3, 2 Cor 3), and a change waits for your Decision line.

## What the sources say

- **Gaffiot 2016** (`python -m pipeline latin macron quandoquidem`): *quandŏquĭdem* only; *quandō* long.
- **Lewis & Short** (`latin ls quandoquidem`): headword *quandō-quĭdem*, but both of its verse quotations are marked
  short: Verg. *Ecl.* 3.55 *dicite, quandŏquidem in molli consedimus herba*, Lucr. 2.980 *quandŏquidem totis
  mortalibus adsimulata*.
- **Wiktionary** (*quandoquidem*, citing B. Fortson, *Language and Rhythm in Plautus*, 2008, pp. 51, 248): the o "is
  regularly short, but may be long when not treated as a univerbation", like the other fusions with *quidem*
  (*sīquidem*, *tūquidem*). Whitaker in our database gives *quandō̆quidem* (either); Morpheus *quandōquidem*.
- **The verse itself** (the corpus, `latin attest quandoquidem`, tiers 2 and 3): the dactylic examples are Lucretius
  18× (*quandoquidem penetrant in eum contagia morbi*, *quandoquidem natura animi mortalis habetur*, *omnia
  quandoquidem cogit natura referri* …), Catullus 3× (*quandoquidem fortuna mihi tete abstulit ipsum*), Ovid's
  *Metamorphoses* 3×, Manilius, Juvenal 3×, the *Ciris*, besides Vergil's *Eclogue* above. With a long ō the short
  *quĭ* would stand alone between two long syllables (*-dō-* and *-dem*, long before a consonant or merged by elision),
  which a hexameter does not allow; with a short ŏ, *quan-dŏ-quĭ* is a dactyl. None of these lines admits the long ō.
  (Plautus, Terence and Phaedrus, in iambic metres, are less decisive; Wiktionary marks its Plautus and Phaedrus
  quotations short, its Terence quotation long.)

## What triggers the variation

Not the sentence: no author alternates by context. The two quantities are two ways of treating the word.

- **Long ō** is etymological: *quandō* + *quidem* written as two words, each keeping its own quantity. This is how
  the dictionaries that build headwords from parts arrive at *quandō-quĭdem* (Lewis & Short, Morpheus).
- **Short ŏ** is the word as spoken: once fused into one word (a univerbation), the long vowel before the enclitic
  *quidem* shortens, as in *sĭquidem* and *tŭquidem* beside *sī*, *tū*. This is how it scans in Lucretius, Catullus
  and Vergil and in the later poets above. Wiktionary's note cites Fortson for it in Plautus (his pages not seen
  here).
- So a print dictionary that gives both is recording the etymological quantity and the attested one; the attested,
  classical quantity is short.
