# *Inquit* set off by commas, and *vēscēris* as a future: changes to the settled text

Proposed 2026-11-14, from the questions in `data/review/exod21-30-stages3-5.md` (Calls worth a look): Nathan accepted
both recommendations the same day.

- ***Inquit* inside a speech** is set off by commas, the quotation closed before it and reopened after it, as
  modern editions of classical prose set it and as the settled text already does about forty times in Exodus alone
  (9:13, 10:1, 19:10 …). Castellio's printers put it in parentheses, *(inquit)*. Exodus 21–30 is proposed with
  commas. When the recommendation was made, two settled verses were said to keep the parentheses (Exod 11:8, 14:4);
  in fact **14 places** do: the 2 in Exodus, accepted below, and **12 in the New Testament**, whose decision is
  left to you (section 3), since they were not in what you were shown. Seven of the twelve stand in quoted poetry
  (`\qt`), where the reopened quotation then begins the next line.
- ***vēscēris***, the future of *vēscor* (Gaffiot *vēscor*; Wiktionary and the Macronizer both give *vēsceris*
  present, *vēscēris* future), where the sense is a command or a prediction: Gen 2:16 and 3:18 (Hebrew "you shall
  eat"), 2:17 *sī eā vēscēris, moriēre* (a future in the main clause takes the future in the *si*-clause), as
  Exod 23:15 is proposed.

## 1. Exodus: *inquit*

1. **Exod 11:8**
   **Apply:** `Exod.11.8` la: `“Eōusque (inquit Mōsēs Pharaōnī) ut` → `“Eōusque,” inquit Mōsēs Pharaōnī, “ut`
2. **Exod 14:4** (God's speech opens at 14:1)
   **Apply:** `Exod.14.4` la: `opprimente (inquit) persequātur` → `opprimente,” inquit, “persequātur`

**Decision:** accept (Nathan, 2026-11-14)
