# Leviticus: three footnotes where Castellio departs from the Hebrew (3)

Proposed 2026-11-16 (Nathan: "Propose the three notes for 13:48, 21:4 and 23:40"). Decision 56: brief Latin notes
where Castellio's reading departs from the Hebrew as read today, worded like the model, Matt 19:24 *Græcē κάμηλος;
Castelliō fūnem nauticum intellegit, doctī nostræ ætātis plērīque camēlum.* Each note sits right after its word.

All three are places Castellio marked himself, in his annotations on Leviticus (1554, MDZ bsb10196330, canvas 820,
right column; the 23:40 note runs on to canvas 821), read on the page 2026-11-16. The Hebrew is the WLC's
(`sources/parallel/wlc`), without the cantillation marks, as Exod 40:20's note.

## 1. Lev 13:48 *sīve in exteriōre parte, sīve in interiōre*

The Hebrew בִשְׁתִי אוֹ בְעֵרֶב is "in the warp or in the woof" (Vulgate *in stamine atque subtegmine*, KJV "in the warp,
or woof"). Castellio, *EX CAP. XIII.*: *Siue in exteriore parte, siue in interiore.) ערב & שתי uertunt stamen &
subtegmen. Ego non possum intelligere, quomodo possit macula in alterutro tantum inesse, cum ea duo ita inter se mixta
sint … Arbitror hic significari faciem pãni exteriorem … & interiorem.* He renders the same pair the same way in
13:49–59; the note goes on its first place only.

Castellio understood the Hebrew (*ערב & שתי uertunt stamen & subtegmen*) and departed from it on purpose: he could not
see how a stain could be in one of two threads woven together. The note says so, with his own *alterum sine altero*;
*pars*, as his text has it (*faciēs* is a thing's shape or look, not the side of a cloth: Lewis & Short; no *faciem
panni* in the Latin Library). Wording chosen by Nathan from several drafts (2026-11-16):

> *Hebraicē בִשְׁתִי אוֹ בְעֵרֶב, ‘in stāmine aut subtēmine’; Castelliō, ratus alterum sine alterō nōn maculārī, partem exteriōrem et interiōrem vertit.*

**Decision:** accept (Nathan, 2026-11-16: "Use version 1, but cite the actual Hebrew too")
**Apply:** `Lev.13.48` la: `sīve in interiōre, tum` → `sīve in interiōre\f + \fr 13:48 \ft Hebraicē בִשְׁתִי אוֹ בְעֵרֶב, ‘in stāmine aut subtēmine’; Castelliō, ratus alterum sine alterō nōn maculārī, partem exteriōrem et interiōrem vertit.\f*, tum`

## 2. Lev 21:4 *Nē in prīncipis quidem populārium suōrum fūnere*

The Hebrew בַּעַל בְּעַמָּיו is read today of the priest himself, "a chief man among his people" (KJV), who shall not
defile himself. Castellio, *EX CAP. XXI.*: *Ne in principis quidem.) Videtur in Hebræo una litera transposita,
legendumq; בבעל עמיו, non autem בעל בעמיו. nã & Hieronymus ita uertit, & hoc sententia postulat, cum hic non principi,
sed sacerdotibus præcepta dentur.* (Vulgate: *sed nec in principe populi sui contaminabitur.*) His reading is given
unpointed, as he prints it.

**Modern scholarship, surveyed 2026-11-16** (the first draft glossed the phrase with the KJV's "chief man", which
modern translators do not follow):
- The UBS translators' handbook calls it the most difficult verse in Leviticus (as reported by its online
  translation commentary, tips.translation.bible); there is no consensus on its exact sense.
- **Most modern translations take it of marriage**: "relatives by marriage" / "in-laws" (NRSVue, NIV, NASB, CSB, CEB,
  LSB, NLT, GNT, LEB "kinsman by marriage"), or literally "as a husband among his people" (RSV, NRSV 1989, ESV, NET,
  NABRE). The Revised JPS (2023) has "as a kinsman by marriage", noting the literal "husband among his kin" and that
  the meaning is uncertain. The NET's note: the meaning is disputed, but it seems to bar burying a relative by
  marriage, including his wife (citing Levine, JPS Torah Commentary, and Hartley, Word Biblical Commentary).
- Others: "a chief man / leader among his people" (KJV, ASV, NKJV, CJB); the Septuagint reads the word as
  "suddenly"; some scholars restore a word for "married" that they think dropped out (the NEB, NAB and NJB read it so).
- Castellio's "not even for a prince of his people" stands with the Vulgate (Douay "the prince of his people"), the
  Geneva Bible ("for the Prince among his people") and Wycliffe; no modern translation follows it.

> *Hebraicē בַּעַל בְּעַמָּיו, locus obscūrus, quem plērīque hodiē dē affīnibus accipiunt; Castelliō litterā trānspositā בבעל עמיו legit, ut Hierōnymus.*

("Hebrew *baʿal bəʿammāyw*, an obscure passage, which most now take of relatives by marriage; Castellio, moving one
letter, reads *bbʿl ʿmyw*, as Jerome did.")

**Decision:** accept (Nathan, 2026-11-16: "Accept both")
**Apply:** `Lev.21.4` la: `Nē in prīncipis quidem` → `Nē in prīncipis\f + \fr 21:4 \ft Hebraicē בַּעַל בְּעַמָּיו, locus obscūrus, quem plērīque hodiē dē affīnibus accipiunt; Castelliō litterā trānspositā בבעל עמיו legit, ut Hierōnymus.\f* quidem`

## 3. Lev 23:40 *perseæ frūctum*

The Hebrew פְּרִי עֵץ הָדָר is "the fruit of a goodly tree" (Vulgate *fructus arboris pulcherrimæ*). Castellio, *EX CAP.
XXIII.*: *Perseæ fructum, palmarum spadices, &c.) Hoc in loco secutus sum Iosephum, qui hæc etiam oculis sæpe usurpa-
uit*, quoting *Antiquitates* 3, 10 in Greek and in Latin (*… adhærente perseæ po[mo] …*).

> *Hebraicē פְּרִי עֵץ הָדָר, ‘frūctum arboris decōræ’; Castelliō Jōsēphum sequitur, quī perseæ pōmum nōminat.*

**Decision:** accept (Nathan, 2026-11-16: "Accept both")
