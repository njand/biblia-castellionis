# Front matter: draft for Nathan's review (final review)

Rewritten 2026-11-13 after your critiques of the third to fourteenth versions; each point is answered in
`docs/front-matter-critique.md`, sections 7 to 18. Latin front matter in six parts, after the colophon and the list of books:
*Ad lēctōrem*, *Dē vītā Castelliōnis*, *Dē ejus interpretātiōne*, *Ratiō hujus ēditiōnis*, *Notæ*, *Librī legendī*.
The three editions share the paragraphs; the variants follow the text. Nothing is in the build yet.

## Biblia Sacra (the whole Bible)

> **Ad lēctōrem**
>
> “Vidēbis, lēctor, Mōsem nunc dēmum et Latīnē loquentem et apertē.” Hōc titulō Sēbastiānus Castelliō annō 1546
> quīnque librōs Mōsis ex Hebræō conversōs ēdidit. Biblia tōta, ex Hebræō et Græcō conversa, Basilēæ annō 1551
> prōdiērunt. Causam ipse in *Dēfēnsiōne suārum trānslātiōnum* reddit: cum vidēret in scholīs Chrīstiānōrum puerōs
> ex Plautō, Terentiō aliīsque auctōribus ērudīrī “magnā ex parte obscēnīs et mōribus noxiīs”, cupiēbat exstāre
> Sacrārum litterārum interpretātiōnem Latīniōrem, fidēliōrem, perspicuiōrem, “ex quā posset eādem operā pietās cum
> Latīnō sermōne discī.”
>
> Nec minus hodiē Castelliōnis Biblia prōsunt iīs quī Latīnē discunt. Hanc ēditiōnem iīs maximē parāvī quī
> grammaticam didicērunt et nunc legendō prōficere volunt. Facillimē enim legitur quod jam nōtum est: quī historiam
> sacram nōvit, ignōta verba ex sententiā conjectūrā cōnsequētur. Vulgāta quidem eandem historiam nārrat, sed
> Latīnitāte ūtitur posteriōris ætātis, quæ et verbīs et cōnstrūctiōne ā classicīs scrīptōribus discēdit, multāsque
> Hebræōrum Græcōrumque locūtiōnēs retinet; Castelliō classicōs imitātur. Accēdit quod hīc vōcālēs longæ omnēs
> notātæ sunt et orthographia ad hodiernam normam accommodāta, id quod in tam longīs librīs rārō fit. Hæc ēditiō iīs
> quoque ūtilis erit quī Biblia pietātis causā Latīnē legunt, Prōtestantibus præsertim, quōrum Castelliō inter
> prīmōs interpretēs fuit. Jōhannēs Ludolphus Bünemann, cujus ēditiōnem Veteris Fœderis sequor, cum discipulīs suīs
> Mindæ sīc legēbat: ūnus bīna cotīdiē capita patriō sermōne recitābat, cēterī eadem Latīnē tacitī legēbant. Idem
> vel sine magistrō facere poteris, Bibliīs tuā linguā scrīptīs juxtā positīs.
>
> **Dē vītā Castelliōnis**
>
> Castelliō, cui Gallicē nōmen erat Sébastien Châteillon, annō 1515 in Sabaudiā nātus est, in vīcō
> Saint-Martin-du-Fresne, agricolæ fīlius. Lugdūnī in Collēgiō Trīnitātis litterās Latīnās et Græcās didicit sēque
> Castaliōnem appellāre cœpit; Castelliōnem posteriōrēs typographī scrīpsērunt, unde nostrī librī titulus. Hebræam
> linguam quōmodo didicerit nescītur; probē tamen nōverat. Annō 1540 in domō Jōhannis Calvīnī habitāvit, quī tum
> exsul Argentorātī vīvēbat; annō 1542, Calvīnō Genāvam revocātō, lūdī Genāvēnsis magister factus est atque in
> discipulōrum ūsum *Dialogōs sacrōs* scrīpsit, ex quibus puerī ducentōs amplius annōs Latīnē didicērunt. Cum autem
> Canticum Canticōrum inter sacrōs librōs numerāre nōllet et dē Chrīstī ad īnferōs dēscēnsū ā ministrīs ecclēsiæ
> dissentīret, illī eum in collēgium suum nōn cooptāvērunt; mox, cum eōs pūblicē superbōs esse dīxisset, Calvīnus
> apud senātum questus est, quī eum ā cōntiōnibus remōvit. Itaque, cum Calvīnum sibi īnfēnsum esse intellegeret,
> annō 1544 Genāvā discessit.
>
> Basilēæ uxōrem et līberōs complūrēs librīs Opōrinī typographī, ut vidētur, ēmendandīs et manuum etiam labōre
> aluit, tamque pauper erat ut Michaēl Montānus, scrīptor Gallicus (Michel de Montaigne), posteā magnō suī sæculī
> dēdecorī esse scrīberet quod vir doctissimus ita dēcessisset ut nē cibī quidem satis habēret. Ibi tamen Biblia
> Latīna perfēcit, deinde Gallica, quæ annō dēmum 2005 rūrsus excūsa sunt. Hæc sermōne cotīdiānō vertit ut eī quoque
> legerent quī Latīnē nescīrent; quem sermōnem Henrīcus Stephanus, typographus Genāvēnsis, irrīdēns mendīcōrum esse
> dīxit.
>
> Annō 1553 Castelliō litterās Græcās Basilēæ docēre cœpit. Eōdem annō Michaēl Servētus, medicus Hispānus quī
> Trīnitātem negābat, Genāvæ Calvīnō īnstante vīvus combustus est. Proximō annō Castelliō fictō Mārtīnī Belliī
> nōmine librum *Dē hæreticīs an sint persequendī* ēdidit, in quō ex veteribus et recentiōribus doctōribus, Luthērō
> etiam et ipsō Calvīnō, locōs collēgit quibus hæreticōs nōn esse occīdendōs docērētur. Calvīnus autem Servētī
> supplicium librō dēfenderat, quem Castelliō refūtāvit: “Hominem occīdere nōn est doctrīnam tuērī, sed est hominem
> occīdere.” Id opus, *Contrā libellum Calvīnī*, diū manū scrīptum circumferēbātur nec ante annum 1612 excūsum est.
>
> Calvīnum ac Theodōrum Bezam, ejus discipulum et posteā successōrem, nec minus offendit quod dē prædēstinātiōne
> aliter sentiēbat: in ipsīus Bibliīs annī 1554 adnotātiōnem ad caput nōnum epistulæ ad Rōmānōs, quā eōrum doctrīnam
> impugnābat, cēnsōrēs Basiliēnsēs exēmērunt, et annō 1558 Calvīnus in eum librum *Calumniæ nebulōnis* ēdidit. Annō
> 1562 Castelliō Bezæ, quī interpretātiōnēs ejus in adnotātiōnibus ad Novum Fœdus reprehenderat, ēditā *Dēfēnsiōne*
> respondit; sub idem tempus, cum in Galliā bellum cīvīle ob religiōnem ortum esset, Gallicē *Conseil à la France
> désolée* scrīpsit, quō Gallōs hortātus est nē quem ad aliēnam religiōnem cōgerent.
>
> Ultimō vītæ annō librum *Dē arte dubitandī et cōnfīdendī, ignōrandī et sciendī* scrīpsit. Eōdem annō Beza
> *Dēfēnsiōnī* ejus *Respōnsiōnem* opposuit, ex quā Ādamus ab Bodenstein crīmina sūmpsit quibus eum mēnse Novembrī
> apud senātum Basiliēnsem accūsāvit. Castelliō ante jūdicium mortuus est, diē 29 mēnsis Decembris 1563, annōs nātus
> duodēquīnquāgintā, longō morbō cōnsūmptus, quem Theodōrus Zwingerus medicus, amīcus ejus, studiīs, cūrīs, vigiliīs
> contractum esse scrīpsit.
>
> **Dē ejus interpretātiōne**
>
> Prīmī Chrīstiānī Biblia ex Græcō in Latīnum verterant; Hierōnymus Euangelia ēmendāvit et Vetus Fœdus plērumque ex
> Hebræō dē integrō vertit; hæc cum aliīs veteribus interpretātiōnibus posteā in ūnum corpus coaluērunt, quod
> Vulgāta dīcitur. Sæculō sextō decimō aliī rūrsus vertērunt, ut Erasmus Novum Fœdus, Sanctes Pagnīnus Biblia tōta;
> quōs omnēs Friderīcus Fūrius Ceriolānus, Hispānus quī dē Bibliīs in linguam vernāculam vertendīs scrīpsit, barbarē
> loquī questus est, dōnec Castelliō prōdiit: “Latiō sermōne loquuntur / Biblia nunc tandem Castaliōnis ope.”
>
> Castelliō ipse, in epistulā quā Biblia Eduardō Sextō, Angliæ rēgī puerō, dēdicāvit, sē operam dedisse scrībit ut
> interpretātiō fidēlis, Latīna, perspicua esset, sed perspicuitātem fidemque potiōrēs habuisse; nam “est ōrātiō
> nihil aliud quam reī quædam quasi vestis, et nōs sartōrēs sumus.” Sīc Cicerō quoque ōrātiōnēs Græcās “nōn ut
> interpres, sed ut ōrātor” converterat. In *Dēfēnsiōne* autem docet apostolōs Hebraismīs cōnsuētūdine, nōn Spīritū
> cōgente, ūsōs esse, neque eōs ab interprete retinendōs: “Rēs enim dictat Spīritus, verba quidem et linguam
> loquentī aut scrībentī līberam permittit.” Itaque prō vocābulīs Græcīs quæ Chrīstiānī ūsūrpābant Latīna posuit,
> *genium* prō *angelō*, *lōtiōnem* prō *baptismō*, *rempūblicam* prō *ecclēsiā*, *collēgium* prō *synagōgā*; nōmen
> Deī proprium *Jōvam* scrīpsit.
>
> Nec dissimulāvit quid nescīret: nōmina animālium et herbārum quæ reddidisset prō certīs habērī nōluit; capita
> Daniēlis et Esdræ Āramæā linguā scrīpta, quam nōn didicerat, ex aliōrum interpretātiōnibus vertit; et sīcubi locum
> nōn intellegēbat, id in adnotātiōnibus fassus est. Eduardō Biblia ideō dēdicāvit, amīcīs suādentibus, quod Anglia
> perfugium esset iīs quī ob Sacrārum litterārum studium vexārentur; et in eādem epistulā Chrīstiānōs, sē nōn
> exceptō, reprehendit quod in dissentientēs ferrō, flammā, aquā sævīrent: “multōs jūdicāsse pænituit, jūdicium
> suspendisse nōn pænituit.”
>
> Beza, Castelliōne nōn nōminātō, interpretēs reprehendit quī Latīnitātis grātiā vocābula ecclēsiastica mūtārent,
> ratus sīc ipsam fidem in perīculum venīre: “intrā paucōs annōs prō Chrīstiānīs Cicerōniānī ēvādēmus, et paulātim
> ipsārum quoque rērum possessiōne dēpellēmur.” Contrā Laurentius Humfredus Anglus Castelliōnem dēfendit, negāns eōs
> quī in cēterīs librīs ēlegantiam sequerentur in sacrīs barbarē scrībere dēbēre. Castelliō tamen, cum quīdam novīs
> vocābulīs offenderentur, in posteriōribus ēditiōnibus plēraque quæ ad doctrīnam pertinent ad ūsitāta revocāvit, ut
> *baptisma* prō *lōtiōne*, *angelum* prō *geniō*, *ecclēsiam* prō *rēpūblicā*, *grātiam* prō *favōre*, *carnem* prō
> *hūmānitāte*, *fīlium hominis* prō *homine nātō*; alia retinuit, ut *collēgium* prō *synagōgā* et *Jōvam* prō
> *Dominō*. Beza factum quidem laudāvit, sed improbāvit quod Castelliō aliīs sē cessisse dīceret, nōn errōrem
> fatērētur; in grātiam numquam rediērunt.
>
> Latīna Castelliōnis Biblia usque ad annum ferē 1780 sæpe recūsa sunt, potissimum in Germāniā, ubi in scholīs puerī
> ex iīs Latīnē discēbant. Sæculō duodēvīcēsimō Castelliō maximē ob Bibliōrum interpretātiōnem et Servētī causam
> nōtus erat; posteā Ferdinandus Buisson (1892) et Stephanus Zweig (1936) eum ut prīmum tolerantiæ dēfēnsōrem contrā
> Calvīnum celebrāvērunt. Doctī nostræ ætātis monent eum nōn lībertātem cōnscientiæ, ut nunc intellegitur, sed
> Chrīstiānam cāritātem postulāvisse, neque tam in hæreticōrum causā quam in Sacrīs litterīs vertendīs et puerīs
> docendīs labōrāvisse.
>
> **Ratiō hujus ēditiōnis**
>
> Veteris Fœderis textum dedit ēditiō Lipsiēnsis annī 1738, in quā Bünemann typographōrum errōrēs, quī post annum
> 1573 in omnēs irrēpserant, ex prīmā ēditiōne corrēxit; eam cum Lipsiēnsī annī 1750 contulī. Novī Fœderis textum
> dedit Basiliēnsis annī 1573, quam Petrus Perna, decem annīs post Castelliōnis mortem, ex postrēmā ejus
> recognitiōne excūdit. Ea ab ultimā quam ipse vīvus ēdidit (1556) aliquot locīs differt, nec cōnstat utrum ipse ea
> mūtāverit an alius; ubi typographus annī 1573 manifestō errāvit, ēditiōnem annī 1556 secūtus sum. In utrōque
> Fœdere vocābula ecclēsiastica quæ Castelliō restituit leguntur. Librōs apocryphōs, quōs Hebræī nōn habent et ille
> ex Græcō vertit, inter duo Fœdera posuī, ōrdine servātō quō ipse eōs ēdidit.
>
> Orthographiam ad lexicon Fēlīcis Gaffiot accommodāvī, et vōcālēs longās omnēs macrō notāvī, ut verba rēctē
> prōnūntientur et fōrmā similia, ut *venit* et *vēnit*, distinguantur. Versiculī, quī sæculō sextō decimō in ūsum
> vēnērunt, sententiās sæpe interrumpunt; itaque textum in paragraphōs dīvīsī, ut in cēterīs librīs fit, numerōsque
> versiculōrum parvōs relīquī, eōsdem quōs plēraque Biblia Anglica et concordantiæ ūsūrpant, ut quīvīs locus facile
> inveniātur et cum aliīs Bibliīs cōnferātur. Carmina per versūs dīgessī, verba loquentium inter “ ” posuī,
> interpūnctiōnem, quō clārior esset sententia, nōnnumquam mūtāvī. Nōmina Patris, Fīliī et Spīritūs Sānctī
> majusculīs litterīs scrīpsī, quod in ēditiōne annī 1556 nōn fit; et quī in utrōque Fœdere idem homō est, eōdem
> nōmine appellātur quō in Vetere, ut *Nōa*, nōn *Nōē*. Argūmenta capitum, adnotātiōnēs Castelliōnis et historiam ex
> Jōsēphō inter duo Fœdera īnsertam omīsī, item notās quibus verba ex Septuāgintā interpretibus aut ex Vulgātā
> addita indicāvit; ipsa verba retinuī.
>
> Restat ut idem ā lēctōre petam quod Castelliō in Admonitiōne ad lēctōrēs petiit: “Quod sīcubi errōrem aliquem
> nostrum inveniēs, dā veniam, et admonētō nōs amīcē.”
>
> *Nathan Anderson, mēnse Novembrī 2026*

About 1,480 words, about five A5 pages. Book titles are in italics and in sentence case (a capital only for the first
word and proper names), the convention of modern Latin; a title inflected in a Latin sentence keeps its italics.
Words cited as words (*genium*, *lōtiōnem*, *venit*) are in italics too. *Librī legendī* is a list, one book to a line
with a hanging indent, in two groups: Castellio's works in modern editions, in the order he wrote them, then
studies of him, by date. Places of publication as the title pages give them (not Latinized), and modern titles
capitalized as their own language does (Guggisberg's English title in title case).

## The variants

**Vetus Fœdus**
- *Ad lēctōrem*: "Hoc est Vetus Fœdus ex Bibliīs Castelliōnis"; *prōdest*; the Bünemann sentence as is.
- *Ratiō hujus ēditiōnis*: only the Old Testament base (*Textum dedit ēditiō Lipsiēnsis …*), and without *In utrōque Fœdere …
  leguntur*. The Apocrypha sentence stays (the section follows the Old Testament).
- *Ratiō*: the marks sentence reads *verba ex Septuāgintā interpretibus addita* (the Old Testament's marks only).
- *Notæ* with the two rows that apply: *oblīquæ* and ᵃ (*ā textū Hebræō*).

**Novum Fœdus**
- *Ad lēctōrem*: "Hoc est Novum Fœdus ex Bibliīs Castelliōnis"; *prōdest*; the Bünemann sentence without *cujus ēditiōnem
  Veteris Fœderis sequor*.
- *Ratiō hujus ēditiōnis*: only the New Testament base (*Textum dedit ēditiō Basiliēnsis annī 1573 …*), without *In
  utrōque Fœdere … leguntur*, the Apocrypha sentence and the *Nōa* example; the marks sentence reads *verba ex
  Vulgātā addita*.
- *Notæ* with all four rows, *Hebræō aut* dropped from the last.

**Sine macronibus** (EPUB and TXT): the sentence becomes "Orthographiam ad lexicon Fēlīcis Gaffiot accommodāvī.", and
the text is printed without macrons.

## English, for checking

> **To the reader.** "You will see, reader, Moses now at last speaking Latin, and plainly." Under this title
> Sebastian Castellio published in 1546 the five books of Moses translated from the Hebrew. The whole Bible,
> translated from the Hebrew and Greek, appeared at Basel in 1551. He gives the reason in his *Defence of his
> Translations*: seeing boys in Christian schools taught from Plautus, Terence and other authors "largely obscene
> and harmful to morals", he wished there were a translation of the Scriptures more Latin, more faithful and
> clearer, "from which piety could be learned together with the Latin language by the same labour."
>
> Castellio's Bible is no less useful today to those learning Latin. I have prepared this edition above all for
> those who have learned their grammar and now want to progress by reading. What is already known is read most
> easily, and whoever knows the sacred history will work out unknown words from the sense. The Vulgate tells the
> same history, but in the Latin of a later age, which departs from the classical writers in vocabulary and
> construction, and it keeps many Hebrew and Greek idioms; Castellio imitates the classics. Besides, here every long
> vowel is marked and the spelling brought to the modern standard, which is rarely done in books this long. This
> edition will also serve those who read the Bible in Latin for devotion, Protestants above all, since Castellio was
> among their first translators. Johann Ludolf Bünemann, whose edition of the Old Testament I follow, read it so
> with his pupils at Minden: one read two chapters a day aloud in their own language, and the rest read the same in
> Latin in silence. You can do the same even without a teacher, with a Bible in your own language beside you.
>
> **His life.** Castellio, whose name in French was Sébastien Châteillon, was born in 1515 in Savoy, in the village
> of Saint-Martin-du-Fresne, a farmer's son. At the Collège de la Trinité in Lyon he learned Latin and Greek and
> began to call himself Castalio; later printers wrote Castellio, hence the title of this book. How he learned
> Hebrew is not known; but he knew it well. In 1540 he lodged in the house of John Calvin, then living in exile at
> Strasbourg; in 1542, Calvin having been recalled to Geneva, Castellio became master of the Geneva school, and for his
> pupils he wrote the *Sacred Dialogues*, from which children learned Latin for more than two hundred years. But
> since he would not count the Song of Songs among the sacred books, and disagreed with the ministers about Christ's
> descent into hell, they did not admit him to their body; and soon after, when he had said publicly that they were
> proud, Calvin complained of him to the council, which removed him from preaching. So, seeing that Calvin was
> hostile to him, he left Geneva in 1544.
>
> At Basel he supported his wife and several children, it seems by correcting books for the printer Oporinus and by
> manual labour too, and he was so poor that the French writer Michel de Montaigne later wrote that it was a great
> disgrace to his age that so learned a man had died without even enough to eat. There, though, he finished the
> Latin Bible, then the French, which was not printed again until 2005. This he translated into everyday language
> so that those who knew no Latin could read it too; Henri Estienne, the Genevan printer, mockingly called that
> language the speech of beggars.
>
> In 1553 Castellio began to teach Greek at Basel. That year Michael Servetus, a Spanish physician who denied the
> Trinity, was burned alive at Geneva at Calvin's instance. The next year Castellio published, under the invented
> name Martinus Bellius, *Whether Heretics Should Be Persecuted*, in which he gathered passages from ancient and
> recent writers, Luther among them and Calvin himself, to show that heretics should not be put to death. Calvin had
> defended Servetus's execution in a book, which Castellio refuted: "To kill a man is not to defend a doctrine, but
> to kill a man." That work, *Against Calvin's Booklet*, long circulated in manuscript and was not printed until
> 1612.
>
> He offended Calvin, and Theodore Beza, his disciple and later his successor, no less by his views on
> predestination: in his own Bible of 1554 the Basel censors removed his note on the ninth chapter of Romans, in
> which he attacked their teaching, and in 1558 Calvin published *The Slanders of a Scoundrel* against him. In 1562
> Castellio answered Beza, who had criticized his translations in his notes on the New Testament, by publishing
> his *Defence*; about the same time, when civil war over religion had broken out in France, he wrote in French the
> *Advice to Desolate France*, urging the French to force no one into another's religion.
>
> In the last year of his life he wrote *The Art of Doubting and Believing, of Not Knowing and Knowing*. That year
> Beza set his *Reply* against Castellio's *Defence*, and from it Adam von Bodenstein took the charges with which he
> accused him before the Basel council in November. But Castellio died before the hearing, on 29 December 1563, aged
> forty-eight, worn out by a long illness which his friend the physician Theodor Zwinger wrote he had brought on by
> study, cares and sleepless nights.
>
> **His translation.** The first Christians had translated the Bible from the Greek into Latin; Jerome revised the
> Gospels and translated most of the Old Testament afresh from the Hebrew; these later grew together with other old
> translations into one body, which is called the Vulgate. In the sixteenth century others translated it again, such
> as Erasmus the New Testament and Sanctes Pagnini the whole Bible; Fadrique Furió Ceriol, a Spaniard who wrote on
> translating the Bible into the vernacular, complained that all of them spoke barbarously, until Castellio
> appeared: "The Bible speaks at last in the Latin tongue, by Castalio's work."
>
> Castellio himself, in the letter in which he dedicated the Bible to Edward VI, the boy king of England, writes
> that he worked to make the translation faithful, Latin and clear, but put clarity and faithfulness first; for
> "speech is nothing but a kind of clothing for the matter, and we are tailors." So Cicero too had translated Greek
> speeches "not as an interpreter but as an orator". In the *Defence* he argues that the apostles used Hebraisms
> from habit, not because the Spirit required it, and that a translator need not keep them: "The Spirit dictates the
> matter, but leaves the words and the language free to the one who speaks or writes." So for the Greek words
> Christians used he put Latin ones: *genius* for *angelus*, *lotio* for *baptismus*, *respublica* for *ecclesia*,
> *collegium* for *synagoga*; God's own name he wrote *Jova*.
>
> He did not hide what he did not know: he did not want the names of animals and plants he had given to be taken as
> certain; the chapters of Daniel and Ezra written in Aramaic, which he had not learned, he translated from others'
> versions; and wherever he did not understand a passage, he admitted it in his notes. He dedicated the Bible to
> Edward, at his friends' urging, because England was a refuge for those harassed for the study of the Scriptures;
> and in the same letter he rebuked Christians, himself included, for raging against dissenters with sword, fire and
> water: "many have regretted judging; no one has regretted suspending judgement."
>
> Beza, without naming Castellio, rebuked translators who changed the church's words for the sake of good Latin,
> believing that faith itself was put at risk: "within a few years we shall turn from Christians into Ciceronians,
> and little by little be driven from possession of the things themselves." Against him the Englishman Laurence
> Humphrey defended Castellio, denying that those who aim at elegance in other books should write barbarously in the
> sacred ones. Yet Castellio, since some took offence at the new words, brought back to the customary ones in later
> editions most that bear on doctrine, such as *baptisma* for *lotio*, *angelus* for *genius*, *ecclesia* for
> *respublica*, *gratia* for *favor*, *caro* for *humanitas*, *filius hominis* for *homine natus*; others he kept,
> such as *collegium* for *synagoga* and *Jova* for *Dominus*. Beza praised what he did, but objected that Castellio
> said he had yielded to others instead of admitting an error; they were never reconciled.
>
> Castellio's Latin Bible was reprinted often until about 1780, above all in Germany, where boys learned Latin from
> it in school. In the eighteenth century Castellio was known chiefly for his translation of the Bible and for the
> Servetus affair; later Ferdinand Buisson (1892) and Stefan Zweig (1936) celebrated him as the first champion of
> toleration against Calvin. Scholars of our time point out that he asked not for liberty of conscience as we now
> understand it but for Christian charity, and that he laboured less in the cause of heretics than at translating
> the Scriptures and teaching children.
>
> **This edition.** The Old Testament's text comes from the Leipzig edition of 1738, in which Bünemann corrected
> from the first edition the printers' errors that had crept into every edition after 1573; I have collated it with
> the Leipzig edition of 1750. The New Testament's comes from the Basel edition of 1573, which Pietro Perna printed,
> ten years after Castellio's death, from his last revision. It differs in a number of places from the last edition
> he published in his lifetime (1556), and whether he made the changes or someone else is not known; where the
> printer of 1573 plainly erred, I have followed the edition of 1556. In both Testaments the church words that
> Castellio restored are what you read. The Apocrypha, which the Hebrews do not have and which he translated from
> the Greek, I have placed between the Testaments, keeping the order in which he published them.
>
> I have fitted the spelling to Félix Gaffiot's dictionary and marked every long vowel with a macron, so that words
> are pronounced rightly and those of the same form, such as *venit* and *vēnit*, are told apart. Verses,
> which came into use in the sixteenth century, often break off sentences; so I have divided the text into
> paragraphs, as is done in other books, and kept the verse numbers small, the same as most English Bibles and
> concordances use, so that any passage can be found easily and compared with other Bibles. I have set poetry in
> lines, put speech in quotation marks, and sometimes changed the punctuation to make the sense clearer. I have
> written the names of the Father, Son and Holy Spirit with capitals, which the edition of 1556 does not; and a
> person who appears in both Testaments is called by the same name as in the Old, as *Nōa*, not *Nōē*. I have left
> out the chapter summaries, Castellio's notes and the history from Josephus that he set between the Testaments. I
> have also left out the marks with which he showed words added from the Septuagint or the Vulgate; the words
> themselves I have kept.
>
> It remains for me to ask of the reader what Castellio asked in his *Admonition to the Readers*: "If you find any
> error of ours anywhere, pardon it, and tell us as a friend."
>
> *Nathan Anderson, November 2026*

## Decided

- **The Apocrypha** stand in a section of their own between the Testaments, in the order Castellio printed them
  (Nathan, 2026-11-13; decision 55). In 1573 most of them are interspersed in the Old Testament, in roughly the
  Vulgate's order; taken in that sequence the section runs Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Ecclesiasticus, Baruch, the
  additions to Daniel (Susanna etc.), 3–4 Esdras, Maccabees (MDZ search; to be checked page by page at M7, and
  against 1551). The convention is the NRSV's and most English Bibles'; the order inside the section is Castellio's.
